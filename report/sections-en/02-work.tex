\section{Work carried out}
\label{sec:lavoro}

\subsection{General approach}
\label{sec:approccio}

The work combines two pieces of software with distinct roles.
OpenFOAM~\cite{openfoam13} is the CFD framework: it handles the mesh, solves
the conservation equations cell by cell and advances the solution in time.
\mpp~\cite{scoggins2020} is a thermochemistry library: given the state of a
gas, it computes the energy stored in each mode, the vibrational relaxation
times and the reaction rates, and it can also perform the inverse operation,
recovering the temperatures from the energies. OpenFOAM solves the equations of the two-temperature model, and
\mpp{} supplies every thermochemical quantity that appears in them. In
particular, the solver does not transport the two temperatures directly but the
corresponding energies, which remain correctly conserved across shocks; at every time step the thermophysical
model passes them to \mpp, which returns the temperatures.

OpenFOAM is designed to be extended through \emph{dynamic libraries}: a new
component -- a solver, a thermophysical model, a boundary condition -- is
compiled on its own and loaded at run time, chosen by name in the text files of
the case. The advantage is independence: the new code lives in its own
repository, the OpenFOAM installation is left untouched, and only the library
has to be recompiled, not the framework. The same independence keeps the cost of an
upgrade low: when a new version of OpenFOAM is released, it is enough to recompile the
library and realign the few points where it touches the framework, while the
model itself stays as it is. Keeping a modified copy of OpenFOAM would instead
mean re-applying every change at each release.

\subsection{OpenFOAM}
\label{sec:openfoam}

\subsubsection{The framework and its one-temperature model}
\label{sec:openfoam-moduli}

OpenFOAM~\cite{weller1998} is an open-source CFD framework: a collection of
libraries and
ready-made solvers that discretise the governing equations with the finite
volume method, dividing the domain into cells and storing one value of each
quantity per cell. It is written so that new physics can be added without
touching its core, and this is what the project does.

For the flows of interest here it already provides much of what is needed. One
of its solvers is built for fast compressible flows and captures shock waves
with central-upwind schemes~\cite{kurganov2001,greenshields2010}; others
transport the chemical species with the flow and solve their reactions. A
thermophysical model closes the system: from the energy of each cell it
recovers a temperature, and from that temperature the pressure, the viscosity
and the thermal conductivity, while a separate chemistry model uses the same
temperature to evaluate the reaction rates. The whole chain, however, rests on a
single temperature: it is the one-temperature model of
Section~\ref{sec:una-temperatura}.

\subsubsection{Where the project intervenes}
\label{sec:openfoam-innesto}

The project intervenes at two points of this chain. Its solver derives from the module for fast
compressible flows, the successor of the solver from which \hyfoam{}
originated, and rewrites only the energy stage: there it adds
the equation for the vibro-electronic energy and the chemical source terms,
while fluxes, density, momentum and pressure are inherited unchanged. Its
thermophysical model derives from the standard model for compressible mixtures
and adds what the two-temperature model needs: the vibro-electronic temperature
and energy as two extra fields, and three source terms made available to the
solver. Being derived from the standard model, it is treated by the rest of
OpenFOAM as an ordinary thermophysical model, and the case selects it by name
like any other. It is also the only place where \mpp{} is called: the solver never calls the library directly.

\subsection{\mpp}
\label{sec:mutation}

This section introduces the library that supplies the thermochemistry and
describes how it is coupled to the solver.

\subsubsection{Purpose and capabilities}
\label{sec:mutation-cosa}

\mpp~\cite{scoggins2020} is an open-source C++ library developed at the von
Karman Institute for Fluid Dynamics. It is meant to be coupled to a CFD code:
it does not solve flow equations, but returns the properties that a flow solver
cannot compute on its own, namely thermodynamic and transport properties,
chemical kinetics and the exchange of energy between the internal modes.

A gas is defined by its species, a thermodynamic database, a reaction mechanism
and the number of temperatures; the present work uses two, one
translational-rotational and one vibro-electronic. The state can be given in
three ways: species densities with the temperatures, species densities with the
energies, or mass fractions with the pressure and the temperatures. From any of
them the library returns all the remaining quantities, and when it is given the
energies it recovers the temperatures itself.

\subsubsection{Coupling with the solver}
\label{sec:mutation-collegamento}

\mpp{} is compiled separately from OpenFOAM and linked to the thermophysical
model and to the zero-dimensional verification programs; the solver reaches it
only through the model.

At initialisation the model reads the description of the gas from the case --
the mixture, the number of temperatures, the thermodynamic database and the
reaction mechanism -- creates the corresponding mixture in \mpp{} and maps each
OpenFOAM species onto the \mpp{} species with the same name. A species missing
from the mixture stops the run.

During the simulation the model sets the state of the library from the partial
densities $\rho_s$, the total energy per unit volume, which includes the
chemical energy of formation, and its vibro-electronic part; from these \mpp{}
recovers $\Ttr$ and $\Tve$. The model then asks for the quantities that the
equations need: the temperatures, the relation between pressure and density,
the production rates $\dot\omega_s$ and the source terms of the energy
exchange. The state is set again before each of these requests, so that every
quantity refers to the current conditions.

\subsection{The implemented model}
\label{sec:implementato}

The two-temperature solver is derived from the density-based OpenFOAM module:
it inherits the fluxes, the density, the momentum and the pressure update, and
rewrites the stage that deals with energy. This section follows that stage step
by step. Throughout, $\rho$ is the density of the gas, $\mathbf{U}$ its
velocity, $Y_s$ the mass fraction of species $s$ and $\rho_s = \rho Y_s$ its
partial density, $\Ttr$ and $\Tve$ are the two temperatures, $e$ is the energy
of the gas per unit mass and $\eve$ its vibro-electronic part.

\subsubsection{Initialisation: from temperatures to energies}
\label{sec:implementato-avvio}

The case gives the two temperatures, the pressure and the composition. Before
the first time step the model asks \mpp{} for the corresponding energies,
\begin{equation}
  e = \sum_s Y_s\big(e_s(\Ttr,\Tve) - h_{f,s}\big),
  \qquad
  \eve = \sum_s Y_s\,e_{ve,s}(\Tve) ,
\end{equation}
where $e_s$ is the energy of species $s$ and $h_{f,s}$ its enthalpy of
formation. The subtraction is a matter of convention: the OpenFOAM energy does
not include the chemical energy of formation, while the \mpp{} energy does.
From here on the solver carries $\rho$, $\rho Y_s$, $\rho e$ and $\rho\eve$,
never the temperatures.

\subsubsection{Species and chemistry}
\label{sec:implementato-specie}

At each time step the composition is updated first,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial (\rho Y_s)}{\partial t}
  + \nabla\cdot(\rho\,\mathbf{U}\,Y_s)
  + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{j}_s = \dot\omega_s ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{j}_s$ is the diffusive flux of species $s$ and $\dot\omega_s$
the mass of species $s$ produced per unit volume and time; the convective and
diffusive terms come unchanged from the inherited part of the solver. \mpp{}
provides $\dot\omega_s$ from the reaction mechanism. Dissociation rates are not
evaluated at $\Ttr$ but at Park's temperature,
\begin{equation}
  T_P = \Ttr^{\,a}\,\Tve^{\,1-a}, \qquad a = 0.7 ,
\end{equation}
so that a molecule whose vibration is still cold dissociates more slowly.
The mass fractions are then rescaled so that they sum to one.

\subsubsection{Energy of the gas}
\label{sec:implementato-energia}

The energy equation is the one of the original module with one term added,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial (\rho e)}{\partial t}
  + \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\phi_p
  + \frac{\partial (\rho K)}{\partial t}
  = Q_{ch}
  + \nabla\cdot(\tau\cdot\mathbf{U})
  + \nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla\Ttr) ,
  \qquad
  Q_{ch} = -\sum_s h_{f,s}\,\dot\omega_s ,
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol\phi_p$ is the convective flux of energy, which includes the
work of the pressure, $K = |\mathbf{U}|^2/2$ is the kinetic energy per unit
mass, $\tau$ the viscous stress and $\kappa$ the thermal conductivity; all of
these are inherited from the original module. The added term $Q_{ch}$ is the
heat of reaction: breaking a molecule stores energy in the chemical bonds of
the products, and that share is taken out of $e$. Since $e$ also contains the
vibro-electronic part, the exchange between the two temperatures does not
appear here: it moves energy inside $e$, not in or out of it.

\subsubsection{Vibro-electronic energy}
\label{sec:implementato-eve}

This is the equation the project adds,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial (\rho \eve)}{\partial t} = Q_{VT} + Q_{CV} .
\end{equation}
The first source is the exchange with the translational motion, written as a
relaxation towards equilibrium~\cite{landau1936},
\begin{equation}
  Q_{VT} = \sum_m \rho_m\,
  \frac{e_{ve,m}(\Ttr) - e_{ve,m}(\Tve)}{\tau_m} ,
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over the molecules and $\tau_m$ is the relaxation time of
molecule $m$, computed by \mpp{} with the Millikan and White
correlation~\cite{millikan1963} and Park's correction. The closer $\Tve$ gets
to $\Ttr$, the smaller the exchange; the smaller $\tau_m$, the faster it is.
The second source is the energy that the reactions take away or give back,
\begin{equation}
  Q_{CV} = \sum_s e_{ve,s}(\Tve)\,\dot\omega_s .
\end{equation}
The equation has no transport term yet: it is not needed in the heat bath,
where the gas is at rest.

\subsubsection{Recovering the temperatures}
\label{sec:implementato-temperature}

Once the energies are updated, \mpp{} recovers the temperatures from them: it
looks for the pair $(\Ttr,\Tve)$ at which the species energies add up to the
energies just solved,
\begin{equation}
  \Big(\rho_s,\; \rho\big(e + \textstyle\sum_s Y_s h_{f,s}\big),\; \rho\eve\Big)
  \;\longrightarrow\; (\Ttr,\,\Tve) .
\end{equation}
The search is a Newton iteration: at each step the two temperatures are
corrected by the mismatch in energy divided by the corresponding specific
heats, and it stops when the energies match to within a small tolerance.

With the temperatures and the composition the library also returns the density,
from which the model updates the link between pressure and density,
$\psi = \rho(\Ttr,\Tve)/p$, used by the pressure stage that the solver
inherits.

In the cases of this project the transport terms never come into play: the heat
bath is a single cell, at rest and uniform, so that $\mathbf{U} = 0$,
$\nabla\Ttr = 0$ and every convective and diffusive term vanishes. What is
actually advanced in time is the pair
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial (\rho e)}{\partial t} = Q_{ch} ,
  \qquad
  \frac{\partial (\rho \eve)}{\partial t} = Q_{VT} + Q_{CV} ,
\end{equation}
the same equations integrated by the zero-dimensional programs of
Section~\ref{sec:programmi0D}. This is what makes the comparison between the
two a genuine check of the solver.

\subsection{Zero-dimensional verification programs}
\label{sec:programmi0D}

In addition to the solver, the project includes four small programs that
simulate the heat bath without OpenFOAM, one for each family of cases in the
reference work: pure nitrogen; the mixture of molecular and atomic nitrogen,
with or without reactions; the nitrogen-oxygen mixture; and five-species air.
Each program sets up the gas in the initial state of the figure to be
reproduced, computes the energies contained in the volume, and advances in
time with a step of one nanosecond, as in the reference work: at every step it
computes the source terms, updates the vibro-electronic energy and the
composition, and asks \mpp{} for the new temperatures. The total energy,
chemical energy included, remains constant because the volume is closed and
isolated. The source terms are computed with the same functions used by the
solver, which is what makes the comparison between the two paths meaningful.
For the non-reacting cases, the programs also compute the expected final
temperature, defined as the single temperature at which the gas would have the
same energy as in its initial state; this is the value towards which both
temperatures must converge.

Two programs depart from this scheme. The nitrogen-oxygen program keeps the
vibrational energies of the two molecules separate, each with its own
temperature, and adds the V--V exchange between them using the formulation of
Knab \textit{et al.}~\cite{knab1995} adopted in the reference work, with the
recommended exchange probability of $10^{-2}$. The cross section of the
nitrogen-oxygen pair, which the reference work does not report, is taken from
\hyfoam~\cite{hystrath}. The air program instead uses a single temperature, as
the reference work does for this case. The programs are also much faster than
the solver: the longest case, one million time steps, takes about one
second, compared with almost half an hour for the solver. The difference comes from overhead rather than
from the physics: even with a single cell the solver assembles and solves a matrix
for every equation, runs the energy stage twice per time step and writes its
output, and the model queries the library once for every species and every
source term, whereas the programs do the same arithmetic with a few direct
function calls.

\subsection{Gas data}
\label{sec:dati}

The species properties used by \mpp{} are taken from the tables of the
reference work: characteristic vibrational temperatures, enthalpies of
formation and electronic levels of the five air species. The vibrational
relaxation parameters are those already included in \mpp, taken from
Park~\cite{park1993}; for nitrogen and oxygen they coincide with those of the
reference work.

The treatment of electronic energy required a decision. The reference work
addresses it explicitly only for nitrogen at 30\,000~K, where it compares the
gas with and without electronic energy; for the other cases it does not
specify, and the discussion of the molecular-atomic nitrogen mixture even
mentions the electronic contribution of atomic nitrogen. However, the final
temperatures reported in the reference work are recovered only without electronic
levels. With electronic levels the final temperature would be
a quarter too low for the molecular-atomic nitrogen mixture and more than half
too high for the nitrogen-oxygen one; without them, both agree with the reference work
to within 0.3\%. The
choice was therefore guided by the results: the project uses two data sets,
one including the electronic levels, used only for the nitrogen case that
requires them, and one with the ground level only, used for all the other
cases.

Two types of reactions are used. Nitrogen dissociation is modelled by the
irreversible reaction $\mathrm{N_2 + N_2 \rightarrow 2\,N + N_2}$, with Park's
rate constants as given in the reference work. For five-species air the
reference work uses nineteen irreversible reactions, fifteen dissociations and
four exchange reactions, with rate constants derived from the Quantum-Kinetic
theory~\cite{scanlon2015}; these constants are cited but not listed in the
paper and were taken from the \hyfoam{} data~\cite{hystrath}. For comparison,
the air mechanism already available in \mpp, based on Park's rate constants,
was also used.
