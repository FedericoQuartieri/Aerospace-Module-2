\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussione}

\subsection{What the results show}
\label{sec:discussione-risultati}

The comparison between the solver and the zero-dimensional programs shows that
the model has been correctly integrated into the solver: the two paths, which
implement the same physics, produce the same curves to within 0.3\% in all cases but
high-temperature nitrogen, reactions included. The comparison with the reference
work shows that the physics is also correct. In the non-reacting cases the
final temperatures agree with those imposed by energy conservation and with
the values of the reference work to within 0.3\%, which is within the
uncertainty of reading the figures. In the reacting cases the final
temperatures agree to within 0.8\%, the composition of the nitrogen mixture to
within 2.5\% and that of air to within 15\%. During the transient, the
agreement is within a few percent for pure nitrogen, for the nitrogen-oxygen
mixture and for air. The largest discrepancy concerns the vibrational
temperature in the molecular-atomic nitrogen mixtures, where it reaches
10--20\%.

\subsection{Sources of the discrepancies}
\label{sec:discussione-origine}

The most likely cause of the delayed rise of the vibrational temperature in
the mixtures is the way \mpp{} averages the relaxation times associated with
the different collision partners, which differs from the reference work. The
averaging method matters only when more than one collision partner is present:
in pure nitrogen the agreement is good, whereas in the mixtures containing
atomic nitrogen the vibrational temperature rises later. The final state is
unaffected, since it depends only on the energy. This hypothesis has not been
verified by implementing the averaging of the reference work, which remains a
possible future development. For pure nitrogen and for the nitrogen-oxygen
mixture, small time shifts of a few percent remain, whose origin has not been
isolated.

\subsection{Lessons from the comparison}
\label{sec:discussione-lezioni}

Much of the work consisted of reconstructing modelling choices that the reference work
leaves unstated or describes only partially: whether the electronic energy is
included, which energy drives the vibrational exchange, which exponent is used
for the dissociation temperature, and which rate constants are used for air.
None of these choices is a minor detail: getting any one of them wrong shifts
the results far more than the remaining discrepancies do. The comparisons in
Section~\ref{sec:risultati} show deviations of up to 56\% on the vibrational
temperature, a factor of 3 on the air densities and about 25\% on the final
temperature. The paper itself provides an example of this sensitivity when it
attributes the difference from LeMANS, in the molecular-atomic nitrogen case,
to a different convention in Park's correction.

Two tools proved decisive in making these choices. The first is the final
temperature imposed by energy conservation, which is independent of any
relaxation model and immediately reveals whether the gas data are correct. The
second is the set of curves extracted from the vector figures, which turns the
comparison with the reference work from a visual assessment into a quantitative
measurement. Finally, verification along two independent paths separates
implementation errors from modelling choices: since the solver and the
programs agree, any remaining difference with respect to the reference work
must come from the model, not from a coding error.
