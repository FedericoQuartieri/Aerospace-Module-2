\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduzione}

\subsection{Thermochemical non-equilibrium in atmospheric entry}
\label{sec:contesto}

A vehicle entering the atmosphere at hypersonic speed produces a strong shock,
behind which the temperature rises to tens of thousands of kelvin: the
vibrational modes become excited, the electronic levels are populated and the
molecules dissociate. These processes do not reach equilibrium at the same
rate. Translation and rotation equilibrate within a few molecular collisions,
vibration requires many more, and the gas behind the shock is therefore in
thermal non-equilibrium. Park's two-temperature model~\cite{park1990,park1993}
accounts for this by treating the translational-rotational and the
vibro-electronic temperatures separately, coupled through a relaxation time.
Chemistry adds a further coupling: vibrationally excited molecules dissociate
more readily, and the reactions in turn remove or release vibrational energy.
How these processes are modelled directly affects the predicted heat loads on
the vehicle.

\subsection{The one-temperature model and its limitations}
\label{sec:una-temperatura}

A molecule can store energy in several ways: by moving through space
(translation), by spinning (rotation), by the oscillation of its atoms
(vibration) and by the excitation of its electrons. Most CFD simulations,
including those performed with the standard OpenFOAM solvers, describe the gas
with a single temperature. The energy received by the gas is assumed to be
distributed instantly among all these modes in equilibrium proportions, so a
single number determines the energy stored in each mode, and the same number
is used to compute the chemical reaction rates. In most applications this is
an excellent assumption, because molecular collisions are so frequent that the
energy is redistributed much faster than the flow evolves.

Behind a hypersonic shock this assumption breaks down. The energy goes first
into the translational motion of the molecules and reaches the vibrational
modes only after many collisions, over a time comparable to the time the gas
takes to cross the post-shock region. A one-temperature model cannot capture
this lag: it predicts a lower temperature than the actual one immediately
behind the shock, and it misses the region in which the gas gradually relaxes
towards equilibrium. Chemistry suffers from the same problem: the dissociation
rate depends both on the collision energy and on the vibrational excitation of
the molecules, two quantities that a single temperature cannot distinguish.
The result is an error in the temperature and composition of the gas, and
consequently in the shock position and in the wall heat flux~\cite{park1990}.

\subsection{The two-temperature model}
\label{sec:soluzione}

Park's two-temperature model~\cite{park1990,park1993} overcomes these
limitations by using two temperatures instead of one. The first describes the
translation and rotation of the molecules, which equilibrate almost
immediately; the second describes vibration and electronic excitation, which
lag behind. Just behind the shock the two temperatures can differ by thousands
of kelvin. Energy is then gradually transferred from one mode to the other,
faster in hotter and denser gas, until the two temperatures coincide and the
gas has returned to equilibrium. Chemistry depends on both temperatures:
dissociation rates are evaluated at an intermediate temperature, so that
molecules with little vibrational energy dissociate more slowly, and the
vibrational energy carried away by the dissociating molecules is removed from
the gas. The model remains an approximation, since the energy within each of
the two groups of modes is assumed to be in equilibrium, but it captures the
essential physics without having to track every individual energy level.

\subsection{The reference work}
\label{sec:riferimento}

The reference work is the paper by Casseau
\textit{et al.}~\cite{casseau2016}, from the University of Strathclyde, which
presents \hyfoam, an open-source two-temperature solver for reacting hypersonic
flows built on OpenFOAM~\cite{hystrath}. The first part of the paper verifies
the solver on a series of heat baths: small, isolated volumes of gas at rest,
initialised out of equilibrium and left to evolve, in which each process can
be studied in isolation. The cases range from pure nitrogen, with and without
electronic energy, to mixtures of nitrogen with atomic nitrogen and with
oxygen, and finally to reacting gases and five-species air. The results are
compared with other CFD codes and with DSMC codes, which simulate the gas by
tracking the motion and collisions of a large number of particles. These are
the cases reproduced in the present work.
