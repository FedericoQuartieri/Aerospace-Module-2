\section{Verification and results}
\label{sec:risultati}

\subsection{Verification procedure}
\label{sec:metodo}

All test cases from the reference study were reproduced using the corresponding
zero-dimensional verification programs. The solver was also applied to the
pure-nitrogen cases and to the molecular-atomic nitrogen mixture, both without
reactions and with reactions initiated from a thermally non-equilibrium state.
These solver calculations used a single-cell mesh, with the same initial
conditions, gas data and time step of one nanosecond as the zero-dimensional
programs. The verification comprises three comparisons:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em]
  \item \textbf{solver against zero-dimensional programs}: both
    implementations use the same physical model and source-term functions;
    this comparison assesses the coupling and numerical integration of the
    model within the solver;
  \item \textbf{zero-dimensional programs against the reference study}:
    this comparison assesses consistency with the published thermochemical
    results;
  \item \textbf{final temperature against energy conservation}: the
    equilibrium temperature in each non-reacting case is compared with the
    value calculated from the initial energy and with the value reported in
    the reference study.
\end{enumerate}

The \hyfoam{} curves were extracted directly from the vector figures in the
PDF of the reference study. Each curve is stored as a sequence of points,
which were converted to physical coordinates using the axis ticks. The
estimated extraction uncertainty is comparable to the line width and
corresponds to approximately 1\% in temperature. Unless otherwise stated,
temperature and species-density deviations are reported as percentages and
denote the maximum over the sampled transient. The programs record results
every 10 or 100~ns, and the comparisons use these output samples. In the
figures, light solid lines denote the reference results, dashed lines the
zero-dimensional results and dots the solver results. The figures retain the
notation $T_v$ for vibrational temperature; when electronic excitation is
included, this denotes the common vibro-electronic temperature $\Tve$.

\subsection{Pure nitrogen}
\label{sec:risultati-azoto}

The first two cases consider nitrogen at atmospheric pressure. The initial
vibrational temperature is either much lower than the translational
temperature (vibrational heating) or much higher (vibrational cooling). In
the heating case, the final temperature agrees with the value stated in the
reference study, while the maximum transient deviations are 0.3\% for the
translational temperature and 1.5\% for the vibrational temperature. In the
cooling case, the final temperature differs by 0.03\%; the corresponding
transient deviations are 2.8\% and 5.1\%, respectively, indicating slightly
delayed relaxation
(Figure~\ref{fig:azoto-3}).

The third case uses a substantially higher initial translational temperature,
at which electronic excitation makes a non-negligible contribution to the
energy. Calculations are performed both with and without this contribution
(Figure~\ref{fig:azoto-4}). In both configurations, the final temperature
agrees with the reference value to within 0.3\%. Including electronic energy
reduces the final temperature by approximately 20\%. The maximum transient
deviations in the translational and vibro-electronic temperatures are, respectively,
0.05\% and 0.8\% without electronic energy, and 0.3\% and 4\% with it.
These results support the reformulation of V--T exchange in terms of
vibro-electronic energy. Using the original \mpp{} formulation, which includes
only vibrational energy in this exchange term, leaves the final temperature
unchanged but increases the maximum transient deviations to 28\% and 56\%,
respectively.

\subsection{Non-reacting mixtures}
\label{sec:risultati-miscele}

The first case considers an equimolar mixture of molecular and atomic
nitrogen, initially at a very high translational temperature and a low
vibrational temperature. The final temperature agrees with the reference
value to within 0.2\%, and the maximum translational-temperature deviation is
2.5\%. The rise in vibrational temperature is delayed relative to the
reference result, with a deviation of up to 20\% in the steepest portion of
the curve. The most likely explanation is the different method used by
\mpp{} to average relaxation times in mixtures. This difference is consistent
with slower vibrational relaxation while preserving agreement in the final
state.

The second case considers an equimolar nitrogen-oxygen mixture at atmospheric
pressure, with an initial vibrational temperature much higher than the
translational temperature. Calculations are performed with and without V--V
exchange between the two molecular species. The final temperature agrees
with the reference value to within 0.3\%. Without V--V exchange, the maximum
deviations are 2.7\% for the translational temperature, 2.7\% for the nitrogen
vibrational temperature and 6.4\% for the oxygen vibrational temperature.
With V--V exchange, these deviations decrease to 2.0\%, 2.5\% and 3.4\%,
respectively. Consistent with the reference study, V--V exchange reduces the
difference between the two vibrational temperatures and accelerates
relaxation towards equilibrium (Figure~\ref{fig:miscele}).

\subsection{Reacting mixtures}
\label{sec:risultati-reagenti}

The molecular-atomic nitrogen mixture was also simulated with nitrogen
dissociation, using two initial states. The first uses the same thermally
non-equilibrium initial state as the non-reacting case. At the end of the
simulation, both temperatures agree with the reference values to within
0.4\%, while the species densities agree to within 1.4\% throughout the
transient (Figure~\ref{fig:reagenti}). The maximum translational-temperature
deviation is 2.3\%. The vibrational temperature exhibits the same delay as in
the non-reacting mixture, with deviations of up to 17\% at early times. The
second case starts from equal temperatures, corresponding to thermal
equilibrium but chemical non-equilibrium. The final temperatures agree with
the reference values to within 0.8\%, and the species densities agree to
within 2.3\%. The maximum transient deviations in the translational and
vibrational temperatures are 2\% and 10\%, respectively.

These cases also support the selected exponent in Park's temperature. With
the fixed \mpp{} value ($a = 0.5$), the species densities in the first case
deviate from the reference results by up to 9\% and the translational
temperature by up to 6.7\%. Using $a = 0.7$, as in the reference study,
reduces these deviations to 1.4\% and 2.3\%, respectively. The
vibrational-temperature discrepancy remains essentially unchanged,
consistent with its likely origin in the relaxation model.

\subsection{Five-species air}
\label{sec:risultati-aria}

The final case considers air, initially composed of 79\% nitrogen and 21\%
oxygen by mole, at low pressure and high temperature. The calculation uses
nineteen reactions and a single temperature. With the Quantum-Kinetic rate
constants, the temperature agrees with the reference result to within 0.7\%
during the transient and 0.4\% at the final time. The densities of all five
species agree to within 15\%, with four species agreeing to within 7\%
(Figure~\ref{fig:aria}). Using the Park mechanism available in \mpp{}
increases the maximum temperature deviation to 8.9\%, while the densities of
molecular and atomic oxygen, atomic nitrogen and nitric oxide differ from
the reference values by factors between 1.6 and 3. These comparisons support
the use of Quantum-Kinetic rate constants to reproduce the reference results.

\subsection{Solver versus zero-dimensional programs}
\label{sec:risultati-solver}

Table~\ref{tab:solver} summarises the maximum deviations between the solver and
the zero-dimensional programs for all cases evaluated with both
implementations. The final temperatures coincide, and the transient
temperature profiles agree to within 0.3\% in every case except
high-temperature nitrogen. In that case, the deviation reaches 1.2\% during
the rapid relaxation in the first 100~ns. This regime is more sensitive to
the time-integration procedure: the solver performs two iterations per time
step, whereas the zero-dimensional programs perform one. In the reacting
case, the species densities agree to within 0.03\%. These results support the
consistency of the solver coupling and numerical integration, including the
chemical source terms, for the cases examined.
